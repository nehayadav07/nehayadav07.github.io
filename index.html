Obligation Copilot

A 60-page contract becomes a reviewed list of obligations, with every answer traced back to the clause it came from.

RoleLead product designer
Timeline2023 – 2024
PlatformWeb · GenAI
StatusShipped in Icertis 8.6

Deliver a monthly status reportArt. 4.2

Complete personnel background checksArt. 2.3

3 of 5 selectedCreate 3 obligations

2.1 Account Manager. Within ten (10) business days of the Effective Date, Consultant shall assign a dedicated account manager…

01The problem

Contract managers read agreements line by line to find what each team owes. Delivery, finance, risk and compliance each have obligations buried in different articles.

Missed obligations cost money and create audit exposure. Extracting them by hand took hours per contract, and the results varied by who did the reading. Generative AI could pull them out in seconds, but a legal team will not act on an answer it cannot verify.

So the brief was not "add AI". It was: make AI output that a contract manager will trust enough to create records from.

02What I learned

Working with the legal SME and shadowing two contract managers, three things shaped the design:

  • Trust is per answer, not per tool. Reviewers wanted to check each obligation against the contract text, not read a disclaimer once.
  • Obligations belong to teams. Managers think "what does Delivery owe" before "what does Article 4 say". Grouping by owner mirrors how the work is assigned.
  • The dangerous moment is creation. Once an obligation is a record, it triggers tasks and reminders. That step needed a deliberate human gate.
03Key decisions

Three decisions carried the design. Each traded something away.

DECISION 1

Every obligation links to its source clause

The contract opens beside the copilot. Selecting an obligation scrolls the document to the exact clause and highlights it. No answer stands alone.

This turned "is the AI right?" into a one-click check, and it gave the legal team the audit trail they needed to sign off.

Trade-off: the split view costs horizontal space. Early versions cut the obligations table in half; I rebalanced it to 60/40 and made the document a focused clause preview.
Source clause· Article 2.1 · page 3 of 18

Linked obligation → Assign a dedicated account manager

2.1 Within ten (10) business days of the Effective Date, Consultant shall assign a dedicated account manager, who will serve as Customer's primary point of contact.

DECISION 2

AI proposes, a person approves

Results arrive as a checklist, not as records. The manager reviews, selects, and only then creates obligations in bulk. Nothing enters the system without a human choosing it.

The alternative, auto-creating everything and letting people delete mistakes, was faster on paper. In testing it produced clean-up work and eroded trust after the first wrong record.

Trade-off: one more step per contract. Worth it: the step is where the value of the review lives.
ConsideredAuto-create all 14 obligations, then edit or delete the wrong ones.

Fast, but every mistake becomes a live record with tasks attached.
ChosenReview → select → "Create 3 obligations".

Slower by one click. Every record was seen by a person first.
DECISION 3

Guided prompts over an empty chat box

Contract managers are not prompt engineers. The copilot opens with prompt cards for the most common jobs, and shows which attributes it is extracting so the user knows what to expect.

Free-form questions stay available underneath. Admins can add and edit prompts, which is what shipped as customizable user prompts in 8.6.

Trade-off: prompt cards limit discovery of what else the model can do. We accepted that for a first release aimed at a repeatable task.
Find every obligation and group them by owning team most used
Compare two versions and summarize changed obligations prompt 2
Which obligations apply after a data breach? prompt 3
Ask about obligations in this contract… free text

Outcome

Shipped as GenAI obligation discovery with customizable prompts in Icertis 8.6 (July 2024), after four design iterations from the first flow to the Sales Kickoff demo to release.

04Looking back

The first version gave the document half the screen and let the obligations table get cramped. I would start from the reviewer's task, the list, and treat the document as evidence that appears on demand. That is the version shown here.

I would also test the grouping earlier. Team-based tabs felt obvious in hindsight but arrived in V3.

Risk intelligence for AI agents

From "what are my AI agents doing?" to "what should I do now?" A security console that answers in questions, and the dark-first design system behind it.

RoleProduct designer (freelance)
Timeline2025 – 2026
PlatformWeb console · dark-first
StatusLive at intentyx.ai
Dashboard· Acme Corp · last 30 days · updated 2 hours ago
Is my organization more exposed today than 30 days ago?
Criticaloverall agentic risk
90risk score
+26pts vs baseline
12new data exposures
On this page: Where is the risk concentrated? · What changed? · What is now at risk? · What happens if this continues? · What actions are required now?
01The problem

Companies are deploying AI agents that read customer data, call external tools and reason with outside models. Security teams cannot see which agent touches what, what changed since last week, or what to do about it.

Intentyx's engine (SMARE) already collected the telemetry: agent inventories, tool calls, data classifications, LLM cost. The gap was on screen. Early prototypes showed everything as tables and charts, and a CISO looking at them could not answer the one question they came with: are we more exposed than before?

I joined as the only designer, working directly with the two founders and engineering, with no product manager. Scope: the console end to end, the design system and the marketing site. Figma was the team's single workspace: engineers built from Dev Mode, and every doubt and piece of feedback lived as a comment on the screen it was about.

02What I learned
  • Security leads think in questions. Every stakeholder conversation started with "is it worse?", "where?", "what changed?", "what do I do?". The dashboard should be those questions, in that order.
  • Agents are graphs, not rows. The risk lives in connections: an agent that reasons with an external LLM and writes to a production table. A list hides that; a topology shows it.
  • Everything looks critical in a security tool. Severity colour has to be rationed or it stops meaning anything.
03Design principles

Four principles became the fundamentals of the product, and the test for every design decision.

Progressive disclosure: detail in layers. Show the least a person needs to decide, then let them go one layer deeper at a time. Nothing is hidden; nothing arrives before it is needed.

01SummaryOverall health in numbers: stat and insight cards
02OverviewGrouped items, one status each: provider rows, agent table
03DetailEverything about one item: expanded connectors, filters
04Deep diveRelationships and evidence: topology, findings
Signal over noise

Severity icons, single status badges and rolled-up counts replace walls of tags. The eye lands on what is broken.

Act in context

Actions sit where the decision is made, not repeated on every row. One contextual button beats four generic ones.

Built for scale

Every pattern must work at 10 items and at 10,000. Grouping, filtering and pagination were designed for growth.

Questions before charts

Every screen opens with the question its user brings, answered in a sentence before any visual.

04Key decisions

Five decisions carried the design, following the admin's journey: connect, monitor, find, investigate.

DECISION 1

A question-led dashboard instead of a metrics grid

The dashboard is a narrative. Six sections, each headed by the question a security lead actually asks, each answered in one sentence before any chart: "Your organization is more exposed than it was 30 days ago."

Charts support the sentence. The page reads top to bottom like a briefing, with an "on this page" rail for jumping.

Trade-off: it is longer than a grid. For a tool opened once a day to decide what to do, reading order beat density.
Is my organization more exposed today than before? posture
Where is the risk concentrated? agents
Which shared components amplify the blast radius? components
What changed from the approved baseline? drift
What is now at risk, and what happens if this continues? data · incidents
What security actions are required now? decisions
DECISION 2 · CONNECT

Integrations rebuilt around health, not setup

Integrations used to be one long table. Every row carried several status and permission tags plus four action buttons, and admins could not tell what was healthy, what was broken or what to do next.

I rebuilt the page around one question, is my setup working? Four stat cards show health first: active integrations, missing permissions, credential issues, issues in the last 24 hours. Integrations are grouped under AWS, Microsoft and Google Cloud, collapsed until needed. Each provider gets one status badge, and four buttons per row became one contextual "Add configuration".

Progressive disclosure · Signal over noise · Act in context

DECISION 3 · MONITOR & FIND

An inventory with filters built for scale

The inventory tracks 186 assets: 100 applications, 74 agents and 12 MCP servers, and that only grows. Insight cards act as quick filters (high cost, high activity, sensitive data exposure, recently added), so admins start from a question, not a blank table.

Every column filters in place: status, environment, application, last activity, external communication, telemetry. One toolbar holds the global filter, column picker and export for audits. A severity icon leads each row, so critical agents stand out in any filtered view.

DECISION 4 · INVESTIGATE

Topology as the investigation surface

Each agent has a topology view: the agent, the tools it calls, the LLMs it reasons with, the tables it reads and writes. Clicking a connection opens that connection's metrics: latency, volume, payload size, LLM cost.

This made abstract risk concrete. "Payment agent → reasons with → GPT-4o" is a thing you can point at and lock down.

Trade-off: graphs get messy past ~20 nodes. I added expand/collapse levels and a minimap rather than trying to draw everything at once.
RiskScorerAgent v.01· Topology
DECISION 5 · SYSTEM

A dark-first system with rationed severity colour

Security teams live in dark consoles, so the system started dark and derived a light mode second. Inter throughout, for legibility at small sizes in dense tables. Purple as the brand accent, kept away from the semantic scale.

Severity uses one hue each for critical, high, medium and low, and the rule that red appears only on the single most urgent element in view. Headlines, KPIs and charts share the alarm; they do not all get to be red.

Trade-off: the first dashboard broke this rule and read as a wall of red. Fixing it meant a grey trend line and a single red endpoint. Quieter, clearer.
Before

Red headline, red score, red badge, red chart fill. Everything urgent, nothing legible.
After

Grey trend, one red endpoint, one red badge. The eye lands where the risk is.

Outcome

The console, design system and marketing site are live at intentyx.ai. Engineering built from Figma Dev Mode with all feedback in comments, so founders, engineers and I shared one source of truth.

Contrast slipped in the first dark build: grey subtitles on near-black failed WCAG AA. It is fixed in the system now, but I should have run the check before the first review, not after.

The question-led dashboard is the idea I am proudest of here. It came from listening to how people asked, not from a dashboard pattern.

Business Process Designer

A no-code builder that lets admins model contract processes with parallel, conditional and repeating steps, and a tracker that shows everyone else exactly where a deal stands.

RoleProduct designer
TimelineOct 2022 – Apr 2023
PlatformWeb · admin console
StatusUsed in the GovCon solution (Sep 2023)
Prime award process· 7 stages · 23 steps · in progress
Contract—Contract strategy—Bid slate selection—BSS round—IFP development—IFP round—Bid evaluation & award
Contract strategy done
Screening letter done
Expression of interest letter in progress · due Fri
Qualification questionnaire pending

01The problem

Every enterprise runs contracts through its own process. A government contractor moves from capture through bid slate, proposal rounds and award. A hospital network does something else entirely.

Icertis needed admins to model these processes themselves, without engineering, including the hard parts: stages that run in parallel, steps that only apply under a condition, rounds that repeat, sub-steps that are optional.

And the people moving a contract through the process needed the opposite of a builder: a plain view of where am I, what is blocking me, what is next.

02What I learned
  • Two personas, one model. The admin builds a structure; the user walks through an instance of it. Both had to see the same stages, steps and sub-steps, or handoffs would break.
  • Configuration is mostly forms. A "visual builder" sounds like drag-and-drop, but each step carries a dozen properties. The canvas needed to show structure; the drawer needed to carry detail.
  • Status must survive a glance. The first tracker used colour alone. Users with older monitors, and a colleague with colour-vision deficiency, could not tell warning from done.
03Key decisions

Three decisions carried the design.

DECISION 1

Stage → step → sub-step, with logic on the step

Three levels, no more. Stages line up horizontally and can run in parallel. Steps stack inside a stage and carry the logic: mandatory or optional, conditional, repeating. Sub-steps are the checklist inside a step.

Admins move things with explicit actions ("move parallel to the right stage", "make a sub-step") rather than free drag, which kept the structure valid at every moment.

Trade-off: no free-form canvas. Some admins asked for it; the constraint is what made processes reliable at runtime.
Builder· BPM for sourcing

Step: Collect bids mandatory · repeat ×3

Sub-step: Commercial score mandatory

DECISION 2

The tracker is the same model, read-only

End users see the process as a horizontal chain of stages with the current one open, showing its steps and their status. Same names, same order as the admin built. Nothing to learn twice.

The October 2022 version showed only the current step. The April 2023 redesign shows the whole chain, because "how far along are we" was the question people asked most.

Trade-off: long processes overflow the width. Collapsed stages shrink to their name (min 50px, max 250px, then truncate), which I specified for engineering.
Oct 2022One card: current step, its status, a next button.

Clear, but no sense of progress or what came before.
Apr 2023Full stage chain, current stage expanded, per-step status with icon and colour.

Progress visible at a glance; still one click to act.
DECISION 3

Status in shape and colour, plus specs engineering could build from

Each status has a colour and an icon: check for done, spinner for in progress, triangle for warning, cross for error. A hollow ring means "active, not started". Colour never carries the meaning alone.

I wrote the component spec beside the design: minimum widths for stage, step and sub-step, collapse behaviour, truncation, fills for each state. It shortened the build and kept the runtime and builder in sync.

Trade-off: specs take time nobody sees. They paid back in fewer review rounds.

✕ Error#F5D6DA fill · cross

Outcome

The builder and tracker underpin the process modules in Icertis's government contracting solution, launched September 2023 with configurable, automated workflows from capture management through contract closeout.

04Looking back

The builder is still form-heavy. Creating a sub-step opens a drawer with ten fields while the canvas card stays small. If I returned to it, I would put the most-used properties inline on the card and keep the drawer for the rest.

The colour-only status mistake in v1 is why every system I have built since starts from icon plus colour.

02What I learned

The products had little in common. The lessons were the same every time.

01

Build the system with the product, not before it

When a screen needs a component that doesn't exist, I design it as a reusable piece, publish it to the library, then use it. A library planned up front guesses at what the product needs; one grown from real screens fits it.

02

Tokens should carry meaning, not just colour

Brand, status and data colours live in separate token sets. Red always means risk or error, a brand refresh never breaks a warning, and engineers pick tokens by intent instead of by hex value.

03

Accessibility belongs in the foundation

When high contrast is a theme in the tokens, every component inherits it for free. Fixing contrast screen by screen never ends; fixing it once in the system does.

04

The system is a shared workspace, not a handoff

Library, specs, questions and feedback all live in Figma. Engineers inspect in Dev Mode and comment on the exact component, so nobody builds from an outdated version.

05

Same principles, different personality

Enterprise needs calm and density, security needs severity that means something, and a kids' creative app needs warmth and forgiveness. Foundations like spacing, type scale, states and naming transfer between products. Voice and colour don't.

Outcome

Three design systems in production, across legal tech, AI security and consumer creativity, each giving its team one shared source of truth for design and engineering.
